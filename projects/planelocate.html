PlaneLocate — finding a microphone from passing aircraft

Every aircraft that flies past leaves a Doppler curve in a recording. Match a few of those curves against public ADS-B flight data and you get the place the microphone was. This page does it in your browser, from sound alone.

Live mode needs you outdoors, or near an open window, under a flight path; a pass takes about a minute.

    How it works

    1. Find the tones

      Propellers and fans hum at a fixed frequency. I take short FFTs of the microphone signal, subtract the steady background (fridges, mains hum), and link the remaining spectral peaks into tracks.

    2. Fit the Doppler curve

      A passing source drops in pitch along a known S-curve. Its two ends give the speed, its steepness the closest distance, its midpoint the time. A Nelder–Mead fit of that model to each track gives all three.

    3. Ask every aircraft

      Any low aircraft on Earth flying at that speed could have made the sound. Each one gives two places the listener could be, one each side of its track: one pass alone fits hundreds of places.

    4. Let passes agree

      Where places from two or more different aircraft meet, I fit the listener position against their real flight paths, including the time sound takes to travel. One place explains every pass; that is the fix.

    Limits

    • Measured on 20 simulated runs: 13 located within 120 m, 2 found no fix, and 5 picked the wrong place, 2 of those flagged as uncertain.
    • It needs passes by at least two different aircraft. Near an airport that can take minutes; elsewhere, hours.
    • Laptop microphones and closed windows lose the low tones. Outdoors with a phone works far better.
    • Only aircraft broadcasting ADS-B and heard by volunteer receivers can be matched. In live mode the page also only knows VFR traffic and 16 common types (Cessnas, Pipers, A320s, 737s, Dash 8s, ATRs), refreshed every 40 s, so a pass by anything else can't be matched.
    • The speed of sound and the wind are assumed, and the listener is assumed to be at sea level; each adds tens of metres of error.
    • When two places explain the passes about equally well, the page says so instead of picking one.

    Privacy

    The microphone signal is analysed in this tab and is never uploaded or stored. In live mode the page downloads the current positions of low-flying aircraft worldwide from a small relay of mine; it sends nothing about you. It does not use your IP address or the browser's location to help itself.
